Fix #21 shipped

The cabin was reading zero CO₂

Bug · a reading the player can see is impossible

With the scrubbers comfortably ahead of four people, cabin CO₂ read exactly 0.0 ppm within a day and stayed there — visible in this site's own before/after screenshot since M1. Nothing physical reaches zero, and a number you can tell is impossible is worse than one that is merely wrong, in the one figure the design promises is real.

A sorbent bed does not strip a gas out of an atmosphere; it reaches equilibrium with its own sorbent. The ISS runs around 2,000–3,000 ppm with CDRA working perfectly, against Earth ambient of about 420. Every remover now declares the lowest partial pressure it can hold, and the best one running sets the floor: 1,200 ppm with the hydroponics rack helping, 2,000 without it. The rack now earns its power twice — food, and cleaner air than the amine bed manages alone.

Decision · a bound, not a concentration-dependent rate

The obvious physical model is a scrub rate proportional to partial pressure, which is what a real bed does. It is also exponential decay, and the entire offline catch-up story rests on levels staying closed-form. A bound on the reservoir reaches the same asymptote while keeping a month of absence as cheap to compute as a second.

A smaller honesty problem turned up alongside it: the ship was delivered below its own floor, so the very first reading would have been a clamp rather than a measurement. It now starts at 1,584 ppm and settles down to 1,200.

Spec 004 RF-30 shipped

Upgrade tiers, so hardware and crew stay separate levers

Attendance gave crew a multiplier. This gives equipment the base that multiplier applies to — three tiers each of CO₂ scrubber and water recycler, rising in efficiency, mass and price.

Water recyclerClosureMassPrice
Filtration97.0%340 kg134,000
Vapour Compression98.3%470 kg312,000
Catalytic Closed Loop98.9%560 kg740,000

Decision · diminishing returns, in efficiency and in value per credit

The last six tenths of a percent cost more than the first ninety-seven, which is how closure actually goes. If the gains were linear the top tier would always be correct and the choice would stop being one. A test asserts both the shrinking gain and the shrinking gain per credit, so a later balance pass cannot quietly flatten it.

Upgrades also cost mass, not only money — and mass is delta-v. An upgrade that cost only credits would be a free choice.

Decision · a hull states its fit-out

Parts used to be installed by the rule "everything whose room exists", which was fine until a line had more than one version — at which point it fits all three at once. A hull now declares the parts it is delivered with, which is also how a real hull spec reads, and content integrity refuses a fit-out naming a room the hull does not have.

The refit itself is not built: there is no money until M2. The tiers, prices and the catalogue query exist and are tested; spending is the next milestone's problem.

Spec 004 #19 shipped

A skill model borrowed rather than invented

"Life support" was never a skill. It is a system — and calling it a skill was the symptom of a model that had collapsed three different things into one number. The fix was to stop inventing and go and find classifications that already exist.

LayerWhat it isTaken from
Knowledge Mechanical, Electronics, Physics, Chemistry, Biology, Medicine O*NET Content Model domains
Skills Operation Monitoring, Equipment Maintenance, Troubleshooting, Repairing, Quality Control, Judgment O*NET Cross-Functional Technical cluster, verbatim
Qualifications ECLSS, EPS, TCS, PROP, GNC, EVA, CMO STCW-style endorsements over real ISS system names

Decision · borrow the taxonomy, do not invent one

These were not chosen to fit the game and justified afterwards. O*NET defines Operation Monitoring as "watching gauges, dials, or other indicators to make sure a machine is working properly" — which describes the tune mechanic almost word for word, in a US Department of Labor taxonomy that predates this project by decades.

The split is the point. Keeping a system sweet, spotting that something is wrong, and putting it right are three different competences. You can watch it work: the ship's medic gets the scrubber service over the captain, because his Equipment Maintenance is higher — and would lose a repair to the chief engineer badly.

Rejected: one flat "mechanics" number; and a longer skill list with "botany" and "nuclear" as separate skills, which proliferates without adding meaning.

Decision · rooms ask for knowledge, not for a skill

A room declares a weighted knowledge requirement plus the endorsement that certifies you for it. The engine room is part physics and part mechanical; saying so in data beats inventing a "nuclear engineering" skill that nothing else uses. An uncertificated hand is discounted rather than refused — a short-handed ship has to stay playable.

Decision · the flow overlay is deleted, not fixed

Three lines in a twelve-unit margin can encode how much passes each deck and nothing else, because there is nowhere to route an edge sideways. Flow views exist to show what connects to what. It became its own view with eight channels — one per Life gauge, plus power, each using the same grammar: sources in, returns back, consumers ranked by magnitude, a buffer absorbing the difference.

Water is why it was worth building. The Life tab reports "86.1% loop closure" and gives no cause; the diagram shows the missing eleven points leaving the loop as plant matter. The footer states the counterfactual outright: recycler offline is −21.5 kg/day and 37 days of tank.

Nothing was lost: per-deck magnitude was already printed in every deck header.

Balance · the specialist was losing ground on her own loop

Found by running the world out to 30, 120 and 365 days rather than by a failing test. Under the new model the ship's life-support technician sat just below break-even — eight hours of tending at quality 0.44 against sixteen hours of decay — so the one person qualified to keep the recycler sweet was slowly losing to it.

Tune gain went from 6 to 10 per day per unit of quality, putting break-even at 0.30. A qualified specialist now holds their system at about spec, a better one takes it past, and an unqualified stand-in still beats nobody.

Spec 004 #18 shipped

Rooms that look like rooms, and a second axis for wear

The schematic drew every component as an abstract mark. It generated cleanly from data and it was legible — but a cylinder reads as a cylinder, not as a CO₂ scrubber you could walk up to and service.

Decision · elevation over dimetric

Projection looks more like a game and costs horizontal space: the same four machines stop fitting across a phone, and every part becomes three faces with overlap ordering to resolve. On a portrait mobile game that is the wrong trade.

Rejected: dimetric projection, and a full-perspective interior.

Decision · condition and tune are different problems

A water recycler does not simply wear out. Gunk builds up in a line. A hose sits slightly outside its specified diameter and nobody measures it. A fungus takes hold in the root system, or a batch of seed needs lower light for a few days after germination or half the seedlings die. Anyone can run the plant; a skilled operator notices — and a very good one runs it better than the manufacturer intended.

So efficiency became a second state variable. Condition falls through use and is restored by a work order costing hours and spares. Tune falls through inattention and is restored only by assignment. A part can be mechanically sound and badly out of adjustment, or freshly trimmed and about to break.

Rejected: an instantaneous "someone is present, therefore better right now" multiplier, which is the wrong physics for a machine nobody is watching.

Decision · a floor, so absence is never punished

Tune decay is bounded: a wholly neglected system bottoms out at 0.86 of rated rather than falling to nothing. Inefficient, never non-viable, never spiralling, and recovered by putting someone back on it. Ships are delivered exactly at spec, so the nameplate is what you get on day one and every change from there is something you did or failed to do.

This cost the earlier rule its literal form — "rated means unattended" became a floor rather than a fixed point — which is recorded here rather than quietly rewritten.

Fix #15 shipped

The flow figure disagreed with the simulation

Bug · a link that was not the number it depicted

roomViews scaled water use by part condition. lifeBalance does not — and deliberately: a worn electrolysis unit drinks the same water and returns less oxygen for it. That is the inefficiency. Scaling both ends would hold efficiency constant and leave nothing for maintenance or a better unit to improve.

So the drawn figure and the spent figure disagreed whenever a part was off full condition, which on a thirty-one-year-old hauler is always. Two tests now lock the selector to the simulation rather than to a constant.

Spec 003 #14 shipped

The ship you can see

Before this, the ship was a list of collapsible rows. Everything the simulation knew was reachable — tap a deck, read the parts — but nothing was visible. You could not glance at the Ariadne and see that three of four crew were asleep.

Three directions were mocked up before any production code was written:

Decision · data-driven art over better-looking art

The cutaway looked best. It was rejected because art does not come out of JSON: every new component would have become an art commission, quietly turning the content pipeline into an art pipeline. What the cutaway was right about is colour as meaning — warm where people rest, green where things grow, red where something failed — and that was kept.

Rejected: illustrated cutaway. Deferred: flow as a competing view.

Decision · crew locations are derived, never stored

A marker's deck comes from activity, work order and declared station. No field was added to the saved state, so there is no migration, no new determinism surface, and the picture cannot drift out of agreement with the roster — because there is nothing to keep in sync.

M2 groundwork #13 shipped

Orbits, transfers and the first four ports

Bodies travel circular coplanar orbits at their real radii and periods, so a position is a closed-form function of time and catch-up never has to integrate anything. Transfers are Hohmann conics.

Decision · numbers you can check against a textbook

Earth to Mars comes out at 5,594 m/s over 258.9 days, against published figures of ~5.6 km/s and ~259 days. The Earth–Mars synodic period lands at ~780 days, and the departure phase angle at ~44°. Fourteen tests assert those rather than asserting whatever the code happens to produce.

Not modelled, and stated rather than hidden: inclination, eccentricity, n-body perturbation, continuous low thrust.

Site #12 shipped

The homepage was being hijacked by the game

Bug · a stale service worker outliving its deployment

The site root served the game instead of the project page. The deploy was correct — the artifact contained the right file at the right path. An earlier build of the game had registered a service worker at the domain root rather than under /play/, and it went on intercepting navigation and serving its own cache.

Fixed with a self-destructing worker at the root that unregisters the old one and clears its caches. It has to stay deployed: deleting it would let any still-cached copy resume.

M1 shipped

The living ship

Five coupled resource networks, wear and failure, work orders, and four crew on a watch bill. A failed scrubber raises cabin CO₂, which lowers crew effectiveness, which slows the repair on the scrubber.

Decision · the ship may shed loads, but never kill anyone unattended

Left in deficit, the ship browns out and sheds loads on its own authority to hold the critical bus — and tells you what it did when you come back. What it will not do is switch off its own scrubber to save power. No crew member dies without foreshadowing and a decision you were given the chance to make.

Decision · levels are derived, never accumulated

Reservoirs are (value, rate, since) and anchors move only where rates change. Advancing a month in one jump is bit-identical to advancing it in a thousand steps, which is what makes offline catch-up run the same code path as live play instead of an approximation of it.

Rejected: a tick loop with a catch-up fast-forward — two code paths that would drift.

M0 shipped

A world that keeps running

Event-driven simulation, seeded PRNG, command-sourced saves, and time that passes at 24× — one real hour is one game day — whether or not the app is open.

Decision · the player is an institution, not a person

There is no point-of-view character. You are a nameless representative of your guild, working an operations desk. The captain is an NPC with her own judgement, which is what makes light-lag a mechanic rather than an annoyance: at Ceres your orders are twenty minutes stale, and somebody aboard has to decide without you.

Decision · permadeath, and ageing

Crew age, take other berths, settle down on a station, and die. They are not a renewable resource. This is the reason the fair-play rules exist in the shape they do — a game that can kill people while you sleep has to be very careful about what it does while you sleep.

Decision · the simulation never reads the clock

Math.random, Date.now and new Date are lint errors inside the simulation package. Randomness comes from a seeded generator; wall-clock time is always passed in. That is what keeps cloud save and a shared universe open later, and it is enforced by the build rather than by discipline.

Solar Syndicate is a work in progress, built in the open.
