Three ways to make the ship a place

The current view is a list of decks that expand into lists of parts. It is legible and it is not a ship. These are three directions for showing the layout, the stations, and the people standing in them — each rendered with the real hull, the real twelve components, and the real four crew.

  • portrait, phone-first
  • 7 decks, 12 parts, 4 crew
  • every part is a JSON record
  • trace why a margin is thin
  • crew are individuals
A

Schematic

the drafting drawing, made spatial

Keeps the blueprint language and gives each deck real interior geometry — bunks, racks, the reactor's shadow shield — drawn as line art from the room type. Stations are labelled fixtures. Crew are lettered markers standing at whatever they are doing.

What it buys

The ship reads as a machine with rooms in it. Where someone is answers "who can reach that failure" without a second screen. Geometry comes from the room type, so a bigger hull or a new deck draws itself.

Where the crew stand

Position is derived, not stored — on watch puts you at your station or your work order, off watch and asleep put you in quarters. No new state, and the view answers "where is everyone" for free.

What it costs

Roughly a day. One SVG geometry template per room type, a crew-marker component, and a position rule. No new art pipeline; nothing an M4 part addition would break.

The risk

It stays cold. Crew are lettered dots, and a game whose thesis is that the crew are people and you are not may want more of them than initials.

B

Cutaway

the dollhouse

Each deck becomes a small illustrated interior. Warm light in the galley, actual green in the hydroponics rack, crew as figures rather than markers — walking to the ladder at shift change. The ship stops being a diagram and becomes somewhere people live.

What it buys

Warmth, and a reason to care. The hydroponics rack is the only green anyone sees for months — here you can actually see it. Crew read as people, which matters more once they age and die.

What it costs

It fights the architecture. Every part is a JSON record precisely so content can be added without code — but art does not come out of JSON. Each new part in M4's market needs someone to draw it, or it appears as a blank rectangle in an otherwise illustrated room.

And it doesn't fit

Four decks fill a phone screen at this density. Seven means constant scrolling, and the ship-wide glance — the thing the current view is actually good at — is gone.

Worth stealing

The colour-as-meaning idea survives on its own: warm light where people rest, green where things grow, cyan where machines run. That is three CSS variables, not an art pipeline.

C

Flow

the engineering panel

Drops architecture for connection. Stations are nodes, the five networks are lines running between them, and the lines move when the resource does. The coupling that M1 spent its whole budget building becomes the thing you actually look at.

What it buys

Pillar one, literally. "You can trace why the O2 margin is thin" stops being a promise and becomes following a line with your finger. Edges come from the resource networks, so the diagram is generated, never drawn.

Where it shines

The moment a scrubber fails and CO2 climbs, this view shows the cascade — bad air, slower repairs — as a shape rather than three numbers on separate screens.

What it costs

It is not a place. Nobody lives in a node graph, and crew chips docked to boxes make the people feel like assignments rather than persons — the opposite of where the design is heading.

And it gets noisy

Twelve parts and five networks is already near the limit shown here. M4 adds parts; the graph does not degrade gracefully.

What I'd build

A as the base, C as an overlay. Ship the schematic — it is the only one of the three that survives contact with the data-driven architecture, fits seven decks on a phone, and costs about a day. Then add a flow toggle that fades the room geometry back and draws the network lines over it. Same screen, same geometry, one switch: the ship as a place, or the ship as a system.

From B, take only the colour-as-meaning: warm where people rest, green where things grow, cyan where machines run. That is three variables and it gets most of the warmth without an art pipeline that M4 would immediately break.

The one thing I would not compromise on is crew presence. If lettered markers still feel like tokens once it is on screen, the next increment is a portrait strip docked to each deck rather than illustrated interiors — it scales, and it keeps the people visible.
