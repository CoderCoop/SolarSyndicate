Solar Syndicate · ship view, second pass

Rooms, not glyphs

The first pass drew each component as an abstract mark. It was honest and it generated from data — but a cylinder reads as a cylinder, not as a CO₂ scrubber you could walk up to and service. Four things follow: rooms that look like rooms, machines you can tap, the flow overlay replaced by a real diagram covering every network, and a crew tab that shows the people.

The problem, stated plainly

Abstraction was doing the wrong job

Glyphs were sized by importance and drawn as primitives, so a reactor and a spares locker differed in scale but not in kind. Nothing gave the room a floor, a ceiling, or a sense of how big a person is next to a scrubber. And with no way to tap a specific machine, the drawing was a decoration sitting above the list that did the real work.

Three fixes, in order of how much they matter: draw the room as a space with equipment standing in it; put a human figure in that space so everything has scale; make every machine a target you can hit.

Direction A

Elevation

The room seen side-on, as a technical elevation: back wall with structural ribs, conduit tray at the ceiling, grated floor. Equipment stands on the floor or hangs off the wall, drawn at its real proportions. A crew member is a 1.7 m silhouette, which sets the scale for everything else.

What changed

Every machine has a plinth on the floor and pipework going somewhere. The scrubber is visibly two amine beds, not a cylinder. The reactor sits behind a wedge labelled as the shadow shield, which is the thing §3.1 says decides the whole deck layout.

Sandoval is a person standing at the recycler at roughly the right height. Okonkwo is asleep in a bunk, drawn lying down; Berg is off watch at the table. Activity reads from posture before you get to colour.

Hover any machine to see the tap target. Every station, every crew member, and the bunk block are all hittable.

The cost

Roughly twice the first pass. Still generated: each part supplies a fitting (floor-standing, wall-mounted, rack) and a shape routine. The room supplies wall, floor and ceiling. New parts still land on the ship without an art commission.

Decks are now ~130–150 px on a phone rather than ~50. Seven of them is about three screens of scrolling — which you said you wanted, and which is what buys the room to draw anything at all.

Direction B

Dimetric

The same room with a floor plane in projection, so equipment has a top and a side. More object-like, more game-like — and, being honest about it, the option I would argue against.

Ariadne Kestrel-class D6 04:12
2 Life Support −9.9 kW

Why I'd pass

Projection eats horizontal space — the same four machines no longer fit across 358 px, and the two that do are pushed apart by their own depth. On a phone that means either fewer things visible or a horizontal scroll, and horizontal scroll inside a vertical stack is a bad trade.

It also makes every shape three faces instead of one. That triples the drawing work per part and makes overlap ordering something you have to think about each time you add a component — exactly the kind of per-part attention the schematic approach was chosen to avoid.

It does look more like a game. If that is worth the above to you, say so and I will build it — but it should be a deliberate trade, not a default.

Tapping a station

Detail comes to you

Today, tapping a deck expands a list of every part in it and you go hunting for the one you meant. Instead, tapping the machine opens that machine, in place, directly beneath the room — so the thing you touched and the thing you are reading are visibly connected.

Three things it fixes

You touched the thing you meant. No scanning a list of four to find the scrubber you were already looking at.

The failure is where the failure is. The struck-out machine and the red condition bar are ten pixels apart, not one screen apart.

It has somewhere to grow. "Show in flows" jumps to this part in the flow diagram. Later: service history, the crew member who last touched it, what it will cost to replace.

The deck-level list stays reachable — tapping the deck header still expands everything, which is what you want when you are auditing rather than fixing.

The overlay question

Split the flows out

You are right, and the reason is structural rather than cosmetic. Three parallel lines in a 12-unit margin can encode how much passes each deck, and nothing else. They cannot show what connects to what, because there is no room to route an edge sideways — so every link goes to the same place and the picture answers a question nobody asked.

Topology is the whole point of a flow view. It needs its own canvas.

And if it is going to be a flow view, it should be every network — not just power. The Life tab already lists what the ship tracks; the flow view should be the same list, seen as connections instead of levels. One channel per gauge, plus power, which lives in the status bar rather than on Life.

What a standalone view can do

Rank by draw. The biggest consumer is at the top and the link into it is the thickest. Finding what to switch off is one glance, not a survey of seven decks.

Show the buffer honestly. The battery sits off to the side with an arrow pointing into it or out, because it is neither a source nor a load — it is the thing absorbing the difference. The margin overlay had nowhere to put that.

Name names. Nodes are parts, not decks. "NTR Preheat is eating your reactor" is a sentence the overlay could never form, because it only ever knew per-deck totals.

Switch channel. Power, heat, water are three tabs on one diagram rather than three colours competing in one margin.

And it links both ways: a node taps through to the machine on the ship, and the machine's card has "Show in flows".

Cost of removing the overlay: nothing is lost. Per-deck magnitude is already printed in every deck header as a kW figure, which is the only thing the overlay was successfully communicating.

Why this is worth doing: water

Power is a fan-out, which a ranked list would almost handle. Water is a loop — the recycler returns 97% of what the crew and the electrolysis unit spend, and the tank only drains by the 3% that escapes plus whatever the hydroponics locks up in plants. The Life tab reports that as "86.1% loop closure", a number with no visible cause.

A diagram makes the cause the shape of the picture. Switch the recycler off and the return edge disappears; the tank drain jumps from a trickle to the full gross draw, and the three-week clock in its blurb becomes something you can see starting.

The number gets a cause

"86.1% loop closure" on the Life tab is the recycler's 97% diluted by the 2 kg/day the hydroponics rack locks into plants — which is not a loss at all, it is food. You cannot tell that from a gauge. You can see it here, because the plant edge visibly leaves the loop.

The footer states the counterfactual outright: 566 days of tank now, 38 days with the recycler offline. That is the same arithmetic the sim already does, said in the place where the decision gets made.

Consistent grammar

Every channel uses the same shapes: sources at the top, consumers ranked by magnitude, a buffer off to one side, returns as a dashed edge, and a net footer with the horizon in days. Learn it once on power and every other channel reads for free.

Propellant is the exception worth calling out: its footer is a budget against a planned burn rather than a rate, because "kg/day" is meaningless for a tank that only empties during a manoeuvre.

The crew tab

A, B and C are watches

The three letters are the watch bill: three eight-hour shifts covering the day. That is currently explained in a title tooltip, which on a phone is explained to nobody. And of five skills and six attributes the simulation tracks, the roster shows exactly one number — mechanics — because M1 only used one.

Both are fixed the same way: show the clock, and show the person.

The watch strip does the explaining

One 24-hour bar per person: eight asleep, eight on, eight off, with a marker for the current time. "B watch" stops being a letter and becomes the block that is lit. You can see at a glance that nobody covers 04:00 except the A watch, which is the actual decision the bill is asking you to make.

Skills vs attributes

They are different things and should not look alike. Skills run 0–100 and grow with use, so they get bars. Attributes run 1–10 and barely move, so they get a compact grid, sorted so the standout value is visible without reading all six.

Assignment, spelled out

Not just "Repairing CO₂ Scrubber" but how far in, how long left, and why this person is fast at it — 71 mechanics turning into 1.05 labour-hours per hour. That is the coupling §4.2 promises, made visible instead of implied.

One open, three folded

Four full cards will not fit a phone, and six will not fit anything. One expanded at a time, the rest as a row each: name, watch, where they are, what they are doing.

This needs the sim to publish what it already knows: CrewView currently exposes mechanics and nothing else of the stat block. Widening it to all five skills and all six attributes is a selector change, not a simulation change.

Recommendation

A · Elevation, with tap-to-open station cards, and the flow diagram split out into its own view.

Elevation gets you rooms that look like rooms while keeping the property that made the first pass worth building — the picture comes out of the same JSON as the simulation, so the ship can keep growing without an artist in the loop. Dimetric buys atmosphere and costs the phone viewport, which on a portrait mobile game is the wrong thing to spend.

The flow overlay goes away entirely. It becomes its own tab with eight channels — one per Life gauge, plus power — a real diagram with named nodes, ranked by magnitude, showing returns and buffers that a bar gauge structurally cannot, and linked both ways to the machines on the ship.

The Crew tab gets the same treatment: a 24-hour watch strip that makes A/B/C self-explanatory, all five skills and all six attributes, and the current assignment with its arithmetic shown.

Roughly four days of work across three specs: room interiors and station cards, the flow tab, and the crew tab. They are independent — say if you want them in a different order, or one of them first to see how it lands.
